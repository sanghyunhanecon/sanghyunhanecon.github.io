Job Market Paper

Shifting Comparative Advantage and the Value of the World Trading System
SSRN

This paper measures how shifting comparative advantage affects the value of the world trading system. In a Ricardian model, the system restrains tariffs through cooperation (concern for foreign welfare) and commitments (tariff ceilings). In the 2017 baseline, governments weigh foreign welfare at 47 percent of their own, as revealed by tariffs they are free to adjust. Removing both restraints lowers world real income by 0.81 percent. Either alone protects most of this value. Larger economies generally value cooperation more, and smaller economies value commitments more. The system's value is recomputed in three counterfactual analyses. Widening comparative advantage raises aggregate value, and narrowing lowers it slightly. A shock's effect depends on which countries and sectors are affected and how trade and tariffs adjust, not on its sign alone. Across these analyses, commitments carry most of the value's response. The projected climate change scenario leaves the system's aggregate value almost unchanged.

Working Papers

The Mississippi River and Agricultural Trade: A Spatial Equilibrium Approach
SSRN

The Mississippi River system is a critical artery for U.S. agricultural commodities and intermediate inputs, connecting domestic production to Gulf export terminals. This paper quantifies the contribution of this navigation system to U.S. agricultural trade and welfare. We develop a multi-sector spatial general equilibrium model featuring input-output linkages, inter-state labor mobility, and a bilateral trade cost technology that admits endogenous mode and transit hub substitution. We find that disabling commercial barge navigation on the Mississippi system reduces aggregate U.S. agricultural exports by 8.92 percent and production by 1.30 percent, driven by severe drops in cereal grain (−11.8 percent) and animal feed (−12.6 percent) exports. The aggregate U.S. welfare loss is 0.33 percent, or $29.2 billion annually, with spatial incidence heavily concentrated in Louisiana, West Virginia, and Corn Belt grain producers. Roughly one-third of the aggregate loss operates through input-output amplification via the fertilizer, coal and petroleum sectors. We estimate that the river’s contribution to U.S. welfare exceeds total annualized federal cost of the system by a factor of eight, indicating an exceptionally high marginal productivity of federal capital.

Trade, Regulation, and the Global Diffusion of Genetically Modified Crops
with Sandro Steinbach, Carlos Zurita, and Felipe Barbosa

We study how the regulation of genetically modified (GM) crops affects global agricultural trade and welfare, embedding a within-crop choice between conventional and GM technology in a quantitative spatial model of agricultural trade. GM technology raises field productivity, yet many countries ban cultivation, imports, or both. We calibrate spatially heterogeneous GM yield gains from agronomic trials and climate data, then evaluate counterfactual bans and liberalizations. Four main results follow. First, prohibiting GM cultivation worldwide lowers global real income by 0.76%, three times the 0.25% gain from removing the current bans, because GM adoption is already concentrated among the largest producers. Second, the gains from liberalization follow a climate gradient, accruing to developing economies where pest, weed, and drought pressure bind hardest and cultivation is most often banned. Third, GM adoption expands rather than conserves world cropland, since the higher return to farming draws in new land and the expansion outweighs the yield-driven saving. Fourth, most of the technology’s unrealized value lies beyond the commercialized portfolio. Extending liberalization to the near-commercial drought-tolerant wheat trait and field-demonstrated rice traits raises the attainable gain to about 0.9% of world income.
